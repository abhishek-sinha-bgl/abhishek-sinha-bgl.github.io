AI and Enterprise Organisational Design

Do the reasons for organising your enterprise the way you do still hold?

Abhishek Sinha · Technology executive and independent researcher · 25 November 2026 · ORCID

Enterprises have spent decades refining their organisational arrangements. Business units, specialist functions, shared services, regional structures and outsourcing relationships have evolved in response to commercial priorities, operational requirements and the economics of delivering products and services. As AI changes some of these conditions, it is worth examining whether the assumptions underlying those arrangements remain appropriate.

Contemporary commentary increasingly addresses the organisational consequences of AI. Ethan Mollick emphasises experimentation and discovering new ways of working, while McKinsey and Deloitte explore changes to operating models, functional boundaries and the coordination of human and AI capabilities.1, 2, 3 Rather than begin with a proposed organisational destination, this article examines the rationale for existing arrangements and asks which of the conditions that justified them are changing. The answers may support retaining those arrangements, selectively adapting them or undertaking more substantial change.

Organisational arrangements can be understood in terms of how they bring together knowledge, work, authority and accountability. The economic and operational reasons for bringing these together have often been compelling. AI may change some of those reasons without changing others. Greater access to specialist knowledge, for example, does not automatically entail transferring accountability; the authority to investigate an opportunity is different from the authority to implement it.

Kyndryl's 2026–27 Readiness Report asked 3,700 business and technology leaders across 21 countries what approach their companies should take towards AI over the next three years. Three-quarters favoured selective integration or a productivity layer over existing ways of working, while 13% preferred rebuilding core processes and operating models around AI.4 These are preferences, not observed changes, and incremental adoption may be entirely appropriate. The question is whether enterprises arrive at that choice after examining the alternatives or whether existing responsibilities naturally direct attention towards improving the activities they already undertake.

That preference makes it all the more worthwhile to examine the assumptions behind existing arrangements, rather than presume that selective integration is either right or wrong.

Do the economics of existing organisational arrangements still hold?

A regional centre of expertise is often established for sound economic reasons. Developing specialist capabilities is expensive, and individual countries or business units may not generate sufficient demand to justify maintaining them independently. Pooling expertise enables better resource utilisation, consistency of service and economies of scale. Similar considerations have influenced shared services, global capability centres and relationships with external providers.

In his September 2026 Brookings conference paper, The Vanishing Advantage of Specialization, Luis Garicano examines how AI may affect these advantages. Specialists acquire knowledge and apply it repeatedly across many problems or clients. If AI reduces the cost of acquiring and applying some of that knowledge, activities that previously required a specialist may become economically viable for those who need the expertise less frequently.5 His examination of US occupational employment data provides preliminary indications of changes in the distribution of professionals between specialist industries and other businesses. Some of these trends predate generative AI, but the economic reasoning is pertinent to enterprise design.

Klarna's reported marketing-agency expenditure fell from $33 million in 2022 to $8 million in 2024. In a separate comparison of the first quarters of 2023 and 2024, it reported a $7 million reduction in external marketing-supplier expenditure, attributing 37% of that reduction to AI.6,7 The wider cost-reduction programme had several contributing factors, but the disclosures provide a concrete example of changing sourcing economics.

AI does not point only towards decentralisation. In June 2026, consumer goods company Dabur announced the establishment and scaling of global capability centres for information technology and digital marketing. The latter is intended to develop in-house capabilities combining AI, data, automation and marketing technology.8 Klarna and Dabur point to distinct choices about where work is performed: whether to develop capabilities internally or source them externally, and whether internal capabilities should be pooled or distributed.

The opposing organisational effects of technology are recognised in economic research. Bloom, Garicano, Sadun and Van Reenen found that technologies improving access to information were associated with greater local autonomy, while technologies improving communication with senior management were associated with greater centralisation.9 Garicano's more recent work distinguishes changes in the costs of acquiring knowledge from changes in transaction costs. AI can reduce dependence on pooled expertise while strengthening other reasons for centralisation, including shared infrastructure, access to enterprise data, integration and consistent controls.

Greater access to knowledge also raises a separate question about accountability. A country sales team able to undertake preliminary legal analysis, for example, does not thereby acquire authority to approve contractual deviations. Conversely, the legal function may no longer need to perform every preliminary analysis. Responsibility for performing the work, accountability for the commercial decision and independent assurance can reside in different places for legitimate reasons. Fama and Jensen’s distinction between decision management and decision control provides a foundation for separating approval and monitoring from the performance of work.15

Anthropic's November 2026 report, Working with AI, describes a commercial real estate company where employees produced and distributed client-facing material using AI without following established marketing approval procedures. The interviewee reported a commercial benefit, but the practice raises questions about the controls governing external communications.10 The report draws on 66 interviews across 58 selected US customers of Anthropic, so the cases offer qualitative insight rather than representative findings. They bring into focus a management consideration that extends beyond the economics of performing work: when capabilities move, which responsibilities should move with them, and which should remain independent?

Who is empowered to explore new opportunities?

Why cross-boundary opportunities are difficult to pursue

The organisation of an enterprise reflects a distribution of authority, resources, responsibilities and economic interests. Within a multinational corporation, country operations may report into regions, which in turn report into global business units or functions. Executives have corresponding budgets and performance objectives. These arrangements establish accountability, but also influence which opportunities individual managers are likely to investigate.

A country operation within a multinational insurer may identify a new customer proposition made possible by AI, requiring a different combination of underwriting, distribution, pricing and claims capabilities. Each function can independently pursue improvements within its responsibilities. Developing the new proposition, however, may require access to people, information and funding across several organisational boundaries.

These challenges long predate AI. Robert Burgelman's research on internal corporate venturing examines how initiatives emerging at operational levels develop into corporate strategy.11 Canales and Caldart's study of three multibusiness firms identifies the importance of collaboration and the distribution of benefits among participating business units.12 A manager responsible for a business unit's financial performance may have limited incentive to fund an investigation whose principal benefits accrue elsewhere. Greater access to AI does not resolve that tension.

What AI changes about investigation

A field experiment with Procter & Gamble, published in Organization Science in 2026, involved 791 experienced professionals working on actual product innovation challenges. Individuals assisted by generative AI produced proposals of comparable quality to two-person teams without AI, and AI helped participants extend beyond their traditional functional expertise. Teams assisted by AI were also more likely to produce proposals ranked in the top decile.13 The experiment took place within an established innovation process. It demonstrates how AI can extend individual capabilities during early-stage innovation, without removing the value of collaboration; it did not test cross-business mandates or the total cost of a business case.

As AI reduces the resources required for some analytical activities, a management hypothesis worth examining is whether access to other functions, sponsorship and a route to evaluation become relatively more important constraints. An employee can explore an idea using AI but still depend on others for reliable enterprise data, specialist validation and operational knowledge. Some opportunities may remain undeveloped because those resources are unavailable. Other investigations may proceed informally, leaving promising work without a sponsor or a route to evaluation.

The authority required to investigate an opportunity need not be the same as the authority required to implement it. The Anthropic interviews also describe employees able to introduce AI-enabled changes within their own areas but encountering difficulties when changes affect other functions. A production planning example describes unclear decision authority and accountability across procurement, manufacturing, logistics and sales. Across the selected customer accounts, the researchers identify a positive association between organisational change and reported AI value, while recognising that change can enable valuable applications, follow them or become necessary because of their downstream consequences.10

Enterprises already have innovation functions, corporate development teams and multidisciplinary initiatives. The management question is whether these arrangements are accessible to people identifying new opportunities and whether they provide an effective route from informal exploration to evaluated opportunity. Defined limits on resources and risk, appropriate access to data and specialist support, and clear escalation arrangements can help without transferring authority over implementation. Existing investment and operational controls may remain entirely appropriate.

LG Electronics’ November 2026 announcement provides a named example of an explicit cross-business mandate. Its new Global Partnership Centre will report directly to the CEO and identify opportunities extending beyond individual LG businesses, particularly in emerging physical AI-related areas. Representatives of its five overseas regions will also report directly to the CEO, with the aim of improving responsiveness to regional markets.16 The changes take effect on 1 December 2026; their effectiveness remains to be seen.

When AI changes what the enterprise sells

Changing the economics of knowledge and investigation can influence organisational arrangements even when an enterprise retains its business model. More extensive implications may arise when AI changes the economics of what the enterprise sells.

In their June 2026 working paper, AI-Native Firms, Hyunjin Kim and Rembrand Koning examine organisational differences between AI-native and other venture-backed startups. Within their Y Combinator sample, AI-native firms have fewer employees, lower proportions of managers and entry-level workers, and flatter seniority structures. The finding most relevant to established enterprises is that AI embedded in products and services provides a stronger explanation for these observed differences than the researchers' proxy for internal AI tool use.17

A conventional IT services company can use AI to improve the productivity of its engineers while continuing to sell broadly the same services under the same commercial arrangements. Alternatively, it can develop platforms that deliver some of those services directly. Knowledge previously distributed across delivery teams may become embedded in platforms, while work shifts towards developing, maintaining and assuring reusable capabilities. Investment authority and accountability for products may also differ from those associated with conventional customer engagements.

In April 2026, Wipro announced a dedicated AI-Native Business & Platforms Unit, with its own leadership, targeted investment and a distinct operating model intended to complement its core services business.18 It has chosen a separate arrangement for pursuing a different kind of commercial opportunity while retaining its traditional business. The effectiveness of that arrangement is not yet established.

Comparisons between AI-native and established enterprises are therefore most informative when they account for the nature of the business. A conventional software business may learn more about organisational design from an AI-native software business than an automotive manufacturer would from the same example. The manufacturer could still learn from practices concerning experimentation and knowledge sharing, but physical product safety, capital investment and supply-chain coordination remain important considerations.

When does organisational change make sense?

The preceding discussion suggests several circumstances in which existing arrangements deserve examination. Changing access to knowledge can weaken the rationale for pooling certain activities. Cheaper preliminary investigation can warrant reconsidering mandates and funding. New commercial propositions can require different delivery arrangements, while activities extending across functions can require changes to accountability and assurance. The appropriate response depends on which of these conditions applies.

Established enterprises have developed valuable coordination mechanisms, specialist expertise, customer relationships and independent controls over decades. Research by McElheran and colleagues on earlier generations of industrial AI in US manufacturing associates deterioration in structured management practices with part of the productivity losses experienced by older establishments adopting AI.19 The study provides a reason to examine complementary organisational capabilities before disrupting them.

Selective changes may be sufficient. In March 2025, following the departure of its Chief Information and Operations Officer, The Hartford expanded its data, analytics and AI leadership remit to include operations while retaining a separate CIO responsible for technology, cybersecurity and infrastructure.20 LG’s plans include a dedicated organisation focused on AI data-centre cooling while retaining its four principal business companies.16 Both illustrate selective changes to responsibilities or commercial mandates without wholesale restructuring.

There is also an opportunity when restructuring is initiated for reasons other than AI. Acquisitions, divestitures, commercial pressures and changes in strategy frequently prompt reconsideration of organisational design. Unilever's November 2026 announcement of a more focused global strategic centre and regional units accountable for performance across categories includes an AI-enabled approach to demand creation, in the context of its wider commercial and portfolio strategy.21 Such occasions offer a natural opportunity to examine whether changing AI capabilities affect the suitability of future arrangements, without necessarily extending the scope of the restructuring.

Seven questions for enterprise leaders

AI can change the relationships between knowledge, work, authority and accountability without changing them uniformly. Leaders should examine those relationships individually rather than assume that a change in one requires corresponding changes in all the others. In many circumstances, adapting capabilities, responsibilities or authority may be more appropriate than redesigning the organisational structure itself.

A practical starting point is to select a specialist function, a promising cross-business opportunity or a planned organisational change, and examine it through these four considerations. The following questions move from examining existing rationales and opportunities towards considering the extent and timing of adaptation.

  1. Organisational economicsWhich of our specialist functions, geographic structures, shared services and sourcing arrangements rest on economic assumptions that AI may be changing? What other value do those arrangements provide?
  2. Strategic opportunitiesWhat products, services or business opportunities are we investigating that were previously impractical or economically unviable?
  3. Decision authorityWho can initiate and fund investigations crossing functional, business-unit or geographic boundaries? How do we recognise and support informal exploration, and how do our incentives and controls influence its progression?
  4. Work and accountabilityWhere could knowledge become more widely accessible or work be redistributed? Which responsibilities and independent assurance arrangements should remain separate, and which would need to change?
  5. Extent of adaptationWhich existing organisational capabilities are important to preserve, and where would selective changes to responsibilities, authority or coordination allow us to pursue opportunities more effectively?
  6. Learning from AI-native businessesWhat differences in the economics of comparable AI-native businesses explain their organisational arrangements, and which lessons are applicable to us?
  7. Future organisational designWhen redesigning parts of our organisation for any reason, which assumptions underpinning the proposed arrangements might no longer hold as AI capabilities evolve?

The purpose of the exercise is not to determine how much organisational change an enterprise should undertake. It is to determine whether its organisational arrangements continue to serve the business it has, while allowing it to investigate and pursue the business it could become.
